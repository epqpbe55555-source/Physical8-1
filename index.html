What is a buffer

A compound or mixture that resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. This resistance is called buffer action.

Buffer composition

A weak acid + its conjugate base (salt), or a weak base + its conjugate acid.

Acid-buffer equation

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid])

Base-buffer equation

Rarely used in practice (bases are volatile/unstable, pKw shifts with temperature):

pH = pKw − pKb + log([Base]/[Salt])

Mechanism

The salt is fully ionized; the weak acid is only partly ionized.

+ Acid: H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ ⇌ CH₃COOH

+ Base: OH⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ H₂O

Buffer capacity (β)

The magnitude of resistance to pH change. Also called buffer efficiency, buffer index, buffer value. Measured 3 ways: approximate, exact, maximum.

Approximate capacity

β = ΔB / ΔpH

ΔB = small increment of strong base added to reach ΔpH.

Exact equation (Van Slyke)

β = 2.3·C · Ka[H₃O⁺] / (Ka+[H₃O⁺])²

C = total buffer concentration = [Acid] + [Salt].

Maximum capacity

Occurs where pH = pKa (i.e. [H₃O⁺] = Ka), where [Salt]/[Acid] = 1:

βmax = 0.576 · C

What shifts buffer pH

Neutral salts — change ionic strength.
Water — moderate dilution barely shifts pH but perturbs activity coefficients.
Temperature — acetate buffers rise in pH; borate buffers fall; basic buffers shift most (pKw is temperature-sensitive).
